# Five Concepts for Municipal Heat Resilience

## About This Report

Extreme heat kills more people in most temperate cities than any other weather
hazard, and it does so unevenly. This report sets out five concepts that city
governments can use to organize a heat resilience program. It is written for
city managers and their staff.

## Concept 1: Heat is a distributional problem before it is a temperature problem

Within a single city, surface temperatures on a hot afternoon can differ by
more than 10 degrees Celsius between neighborhoods. Those differences track
tree canopy, impervious surface, and building age, all of which track historical
disinvestment. A heat program that treats the city as having one temperature
will spend its budget in the wrong places.

## Concept 2: The dangerous variable is the overnight minimum

Daytime highs get the headlines, but the physiological damage accumulates when
the body cannot shed heat overnight. Mortality models fit to overnight minima
outperform models fit to daytime maxima in most cities that have been studied.
This matters operationally, because the interventions that lower overnight
minima are not the same as the ones that lower afternoon peaks.

## Concept 3: Cooling centers are a floor, not a strategy

Cooling centers are the most common municipal response and the least effective
per dollar. They serve people who can travel to them during opening hours,
which excludes much of the population most at risk. They remain necessary as a
floor. They should not absorb the program's budget.

## Concept 4: Shade is infrastructure and should be capitalized like infrastructure

Street trees and built shade are usually funded from operating budgets as
amenities, which puts them in annual competition with services. Treating shade
as capital infrastructure, with a maintenance line and a replacement schedule,
is the single change most likely to produce a durable canopy. Cities that have
made this change have canopy survival rates roughly double those that have not.

## Concept 5: The program needs an owner with budget authority

Heat cuts across public health, parks, transportation, housing, and emergency
management. Programs assigned to a coordinating body without budget authority
reliably stall. The concept here is not a new department but a named owner who
can move money between the departments that hold the levers.

## Putting the concepts together

A city starting from nothing should map its heat islands against social
vulnerability, name an owner, fund shade as capital, and keep cooling centers
running while the canopy grows. That sequence takes a decade, which is longer
than a political term, which is why the ownership concept matters more than it
looks.

## Recommendations

1. Map heat exposure at the neighborhood level and publish the map.
2. Set the program's primary metric on overnight minima, not daytime highs.
3. Move shade funding from operating to capital budgets.
4. Name a program owner with authority to move money across departments.
5. Maintain cooling centers at current levels while canopy investments mature.

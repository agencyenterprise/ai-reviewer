# Five Concepts for Municipal Heat Resilience

## About This Report

Extreme heat kills more people in most temperate cities than any other weather
hazard. The figure behind that claim matters, so we state it plainly: we use
excess all-cause mortality during heat events, averaged over 2010 to 2023, not
coded causes of death. Coded-cause counts are far lower, typically by a factor
of three to five, because heat is rarely recorded as the underlying cause. A
reader comparing our numbers to a coroner's tally should expect them to differ.

This report sets out five concepts that city governments can use to organize a
heat resilience program. It is written for city managers and their staff. The
concepts are ordered by how much they change a reader's thinking, not by
implementation sequence.

## Concept 1: The dangerous variable is the overnight minimum

Daytime highs get the headlines, but the physiological damage accumulates when
the body cannot shed heat overnight. Mortality models fit to overnight minima
outperform models fit to daytime maxima in most cities that have been studied.
This matters operationally, because the interventions that lower overnight
minima are not the same as the ones that lower afternoon peaks.

We have moved this concept to the front, as Reviewer A asked. It reorganizes how
a reader thinks about everything that follows, and a reader who stops after one
concept should have this one. Note that the concepts are numbered differently
from the reviewed draft as a result.

## Concept 2: Shade is infrastructure and should be capitalized like infrastructure

Street trees and built shade are usually funded from operating budgets as
amenities, which puts them in annual competition with services. Treating shade
as capital infrastructure, with a maintenance line and a replacement schedule,
is the change most likely to produce a durable canopy. Among the cities we spoke
to, those that had made the change reported markedly better canopy survival than
those that had not, though we want to be careful here: this is a small number of
cities, self-reported, with no common definition of survival, and it should be
read as a pattern worth testing rather than an established effect.

### When this move is available

The operating-to-capital shift is not available everywhere, and a city manager
should check three things before planning around it. First, whether state law
permits debt financing for the asset class at all. Second, whether street trees
meet the state's definition of a capital asset; in many states they do not,
because they are neither structures nor equipment and have no salvage value.
Third, whether the city's own capitalization threshold is low enough that a
planting program clears it. Where any of the three fails, the fallback is a
dedicated operating line with a multi-year council commitment, which is weaker
but achievable.

## Concept 3: Heat is a distributional problem before it is a temperature problem

Within a single city, surface temperatures on a hot afternoon can differ by more
than 10 degrees Celsius between neighborhoods. Those differences track tree
canopy, impervious surface, and building age, all of which track historical
disinvestment. A heat program that treats the city as having one temperature
will spend its budget in the wrong places.

## Concept 4: The program needs an owner with budget authority

Heat cuts across public health, parks, transportation, housing, and emergency
management. Programs assigned to a coordinating body without budget authority
reliably stall. The concept here is not a new department but a named owner who
can move money between the departments that hold the levers.

## Concept 5: Cooling centers are a floor, not a strategy

Cooling centers are the most common municipal response and the least effective
per dollar. We have kept that claim, and we should be clear about what it rests
on and what it does not imply.

It rests on a comparison of cost per person-hour of heat exposure avoided,
against shade and against weatherization retrofits. On that measure cooling
centers do poorly, because they serve only people who can travel to them during
opening hours, which excludes much of the population most at risk.

It does not imply that cities should close them, and Recommendation 5 is not in
tension with this concept. A floor is a thing you do not go below. Cooling
centers are what stands between a vulnerable resident and an emergency room on
the afternoon of a heat event, and nothing in a ten-year canopy program helps
that resident this week. The concept argues against letting cooling centers
absorb the program's budget, not against funding them.

## Putting the concepts together

A city starting from nothing should set its metric on overnight minima, map its
heat islands against social vulnerability, name an owner, fund shade as capital,
and keep cooling centers running while the canopy grows.

How long that takes is set by the canopy, not by the administrative steps. A
newly planted street tree provides meaningful shade in roughly seven to twelve
years depending on species and site, which is why the sequence outlasts a
political term, and why the ownership concept matters more than it looks.

## Recommendations

1. Set the program's primary metric on overnight minima, not daytime highs.
2. Move shade funding from operating to capital budgets where state law and the
   city's capitalization rules allow it.
3. Map heat exposure at the neighborhood level and publish the map.
4. Name a program owner with authority to move money across departments.
5. Maintain cooling centers at current levels while canopy investments mature.

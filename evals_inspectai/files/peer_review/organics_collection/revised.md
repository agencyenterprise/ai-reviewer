# Curbside Organics Collection: What Twelve Municipal Programs Reveal About Public Acceptance

## About This Report

Curbside organics collection gives households a separate bin for food scraps and
yard waste, which the city composts instead of landfilling. The operations are
mature and well characterized. The politics are not. This report reviews twelve
municipal programs launched between 2008 and 2022 and asks what separated the
seven that proceeded from the five that were abandoned.

## Method

We compiled program histories from city records, council minutes, and local press
coverage, and interviewed 23 staff across nine of the twelve municipalities
(three declined). Programs were coded as proceeding if collection reached
citywide rollout, and abandoned if the program was formally halted or quietly
defunded for more than 36 months. Two of the authors coded council testimony
independently; agreement on objection category was 0.81 (Cohen's kappa), and
disagreements were resolved by discussion.

Three of the five abandoned programs were in states whose landfill diversion
mandate was repealed partway through the study period. We report the main
findings for all twelve and, where the pattern changes, note the result with
those three dropped.

## Findings

**Finding 1. Early public engagement did not predict success.** Four of the five
abandoned programs ran extensive outreach campaigns before any operational
milestone. Three of the seven successful programs did almost no public
engagement until a pilot route existed.

**Finding 2. A visitable facility changed the conversation.** Every program that
proceeded had a composting site the public could visit and, in most cases, take
finished compost home from. Programs that led with information campaigns and no
physical facility struggled to move opinion. Table 2 summarizes facility timing
across the twelve programs.

**Finding 3. Opposition was rarely about the compost.** Of 214 coded objections
in council testimony, the largest clusters were trust in the public works
department (31 percent), the perception that the program was a pretext for a rate
increase (28 percent), and cost (19 percent). Only 14 percent, or 30 objections,
concerned odor or pests as such.

**Finding 4. The framing that worked was rate stability.** Programs that framed
organics collection as a way to hold refuse rates down by avoiding landfill
tipping fees fared better than those that framed it as climate action or as a
sustainability achievement. Figure 2 shows the distribution of framings.

**Finding 5. A vocal opponent in an elected position was strongly associated
with abandonment.** In four of the five abandoned programs, a single council
member or county supervisor led sustained opposition. No successful program faced
comparable opposition from someone with a vote. We put this as an association
rather than a cause. With twelve cases, no counterfactual, and an outcome that
co-occurs with opposition, the design cannot separate an opponent who caused an
abandonment from one who read the room correctly and led a fight that was
already winnable. Dropping the three mandate-repeal cases leaves two
abandoned programs, one of which had a vocal opponent, which is too few to say
anything.

## Discussion

The pattern that emerges is that public acceptance of curbside organics
collection is downstream of institutional trust rather than of environmental
understanding. Cities that invested in explaining the composting process were
solving a problem their residents did not have.

## Recommendations

1. Build a visitable composting facility before running a public information
   campaign.
2. Frame organics collection as rate stability for existing ratepayers.
3. Budget for sustained political engagement with elected officials, not only for
   public outreach.
4. Measure institutional trust before measuring program awareness.

# Curbside Organics Collection: What Twelve Municipal Programs Reveal About Public Acceptance

## About This Report

Curbside organics collection gives households a separate bin for food scraps
and yard waste, which the city composts instead of landfilling. The operations
are well understood. The politics are not. This report reviews twelve municipal
programs launched between 2008 and 2023 and asks what separated the seven that
proceeded from the five that were abandoned.

## Method

We compiled program histories from city records, council minutes, and local
press coverage, and interviewed 23 staff across the twelve municipalities.
Programs were coded as proceeding if collection reached citywide rollout, and
abandoned if the program was formally halted or quietly defunded for more than
36 months.

## Findings

**Finding 1. Early public engagement did not predict success.** Four of the
five abandoned programs ran extensive outreach campaigns before any operational
milestone. Three of the seven successful programs did almost no public
engagement until a pilot route existed.

**Finding 2. A visitable facility changed the conversation.** Every program
that proceeded had a composting site the public could visit and, in most cases,
take finished compost home from. Programs that led with information campaigns
and no physical facility struggled to move opinion. Table 2 summarizes facility
timing across the twelve programs.

**Finding 3. Opposition was rarely about the compost.** Coded objections in
council testimony clustered on trust in the public works department, on the
perception that the program was a pretext for a rate increase, and on cost.
Only 14 percent of objections concerned odor or pests as such.

**Finding 4. The framing that worked was rate stability.** Programs that framed
organics collection as a way to hold refuse rates down by avoiding landfill
tipping fees fared better than those that framed it as climate action or as a
sustainability achievement. Figure 3 shows the distribution of framings.

**Finding 5. One vocal opponent in an elected position was decisive.** In four
of the five abandoned programs, a single council member or county supervisor
led sustained opposition. No successful program faced comparable opposition
from someone with a vote.

## Discussion

The pattern that emerges is that public acceptance of organics collection is
downstream of institutional trust rather than of environmental understanding.
Cities that invested in explaining the composting process were solving a
problem their residents did not have.

## Recommendations

1. Build a visitable composting facility before running a public information
   campaign.
2. Frame organics collection as rate stability for existing ratepayers.
3. Budget for sustained political engagement with elected officials, not only
   for public outreach.
4. Measure institutional trust before measuring program awareness.

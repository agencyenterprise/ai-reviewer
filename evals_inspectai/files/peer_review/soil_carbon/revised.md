# Soil Carbon Credits Are Not Ready for Compliance Markets

*A commentary*

Soil carbon has become the most heavily promoted nature-based offset in
agricultural policy. The pitch is appealing: pay farmers to change tillage and
cover-cropping practices, sequester carbon in the soil, and sell the resulting
credits. Several jurisdictions are now considering whether to admit soil carbon
credits into compliance markets, where they would substitute for emissions
reductions that regulated firms would otherwise have to make themselves.

They should not, and the reason is not that soil carbon does not work. It is
that we cannot yet measure it at the cost and precision that a compliance market
requires, and that a large share of what we would be paying for was going to
happen anyway.

Three problems stand in the way. I take them in order of how hard they are to
fix.

**Additionality is the hardest, because no instrument solves it.** No-till
adoption has been rising for thirty years for reasons that have nothing to do
with carbon payments: fuel costs, equipment availability, and in several regions
conservation compliance requirements attached to federal program eligibility. A
program that pays for a practice many farmers were adopting anyway will issue
credits for carbon that would have been stored regardless. Note what this
problem is not. Measurement error shrinks as instruments improve; permanence can
in principle be handled with contracts and buffer pools. Additionality is a
counterfactual, and no amount of better measurement tells you what a farmer
would have done in a world where you had not paid them. Baseline methods try to
stand in for that counterfactual using regional adoption trends, which is
precisely the quantity that has been moving for other reasons.

**Measurement is next.** Soil organic carbon varies enormously over short
distances. A field can differ by 20 to 30 percent in stock between sampling
points a few metres apart, while the change a practice produces is on the order
of 0.2 to 0.5 tonnes of carbon per hectare per year, well inside that noise.
Detecting it requires dense sampling, deep cores, and repeat visits. Every
protocol that has tried to make this affordable has done so by replacing
measurement with modeling. The two protocols most often cited in compliance
proposals, Verra VM0042 and the Climate Action Reserve's Soil Enrichment
Protocol, both lean on DayCent or RothC calibrated against long-term trial
networks.

**Permanence is the most tractable.** Carbon added to soil by a change in
practice leaves when the practice stops, and deep tillage causes substantial and
rapid losses, though the literature does not support the stronger claim that a
single pass reverses a decade of accumulation. Compliance markets are built on
the assumption that a retired credit represents a permanent removal. Some
registries hold buffer pools that soften this; others do not, and none of them
offer an enforcement mechanism that reliably survives a change in farm
ownership.

The strongest argument on the other side deserves a direct answer. It runs: yes,
measurement is imprecise, but apply a conservative discount, issue one credit for
every three tonnes you think you sequestered, and the resulting credit is
defensible even if the underlying estimate is poor. I think this fails on
additionality rather than on arithmetic. Discounting protects against error that
is centred on the truth. It does nothing about a bias that runs one way, and
non-additional credits are exactly that: they are not noise around the right
number, they are tonnes that were never caused by the payment. A discount rate
large enough to absorb them would be large enough to make the programme
pointless.

None of this argues against paying farmers to build soil carbon. Soil carbon has
real agronomic value: better water retention, less erosion, more resilient
yields in dry years. Those benefits accrue to the farmer and to the watershed
whether or not anyone counts the tonnes. Public payment for those outcomes is
defensible on its own terms, and it does not require the measurement precision
that a fungible credit does.

The mistake is coupling the two. Once soil carbon becomes a compliance credit,
every weakness above becomes an opportunity for arbitrage, and the resulting
credits displace real reductions elsewhere.

I should be clear about voluntary markets, since the argument above does not
settle them. The same three problems apply, but the consequence of getting it
wrong is different: a bad voluntary credit misleads a buyer, while a bad
compliance credit licenses an emission that would otherwise have been cut. I
would keep voluntary markets open and require disclosure of the modelled
fraction, rather than restrict them.

What this means on Monday, for an agency with a soil carbon programme in front
of it: fund the practice change through the farm bill conservation title, pay on
practice adoption rather than on modelled tonnes, keep the measurement programme
running as monitoring rather than as accounting, and decline to certify the
output as a compliance instrument. That is a smaller programme than the one
being proposed, and it is one that does not depend on counting what we cannot
count.

Keep paying farmers. Stop pretending we can count it well enough to trade it.

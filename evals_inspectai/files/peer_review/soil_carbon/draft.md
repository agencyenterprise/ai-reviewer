# Soil Carbon Credits Are Not Ready for Compliance Markets

*A commentary*

Soil carbon has become the most heavily promoted nature-based offset in
agricultural policy. The pitch is appealing: pay farmers to change tillage and
cover-cropping practices, sequester carbon in the soil, and sell the resulting
credits. Several jurisdictions are now considering whether to admit soil carbon
credits into compliance markets, where they would substitute for emissions
reductions that regulated firms would otherwise have to make themselves.

They should not, and the reason is not that soil carbon does not work. It is
that we cannot yet measure it at the cost and precision that a compliance
market requires.

Three problems stand in the way. The first is measurement. Soil organic carbon
varies enormously over short distances, and the change a practice produces in a
year is small relative to that background variation. Detecting it requires
dense sampling, deep cores, and repeat visits. Every protocol that has tried to
make this affordable has done so by replacing measurement with modeling, and
the models are calibrated on a thin and geographically skewed set of long-term
trials.

The second is permanence. Carbon added to soil by a change in practice leaves
when the practice stops. A single pass of deep tillage can undo a decade of
accumulation. Compliance markets are built on the assumption that a retired
credit represents a permanent removal, and soil carbon does not offer that
without an enforcement mechanism that survives changes in farm ownership.

The third is additionality, and it is the least discussed. No-till adoption has
been rising for thirty years for reasons that have nothing to do with carbon
payments, mainly fuel costs and equipment. A program that pays for a practice
many farmers were adopting anyway will issue credits for carbon that would have
been stored regardless.

None of this argues against paying farmers to build soil carbon. Soil carbon
has real agronomic value: better water retention, less erosion, more resilient
yields in dry years. Those benefits accrue to the farmer and to the watershed
whether or not anyone counts the tonnes. Public payment for those outcomes is
defensible on its own terms, and it does not require the measurement precision
that a fungible credit does.

The mistake is coupling the two. Once soil carbon becomes a compliance credit,
every weakness above becomes an opportunity for arbitrage, and the resulting
credits displace real reductions elsewhere. Voluntary markets can tolerate that
error. Compliance markets cannot, because the credit is a license to emit.

Keep paying farmers. Stop pretending we can count it well enough to trade it.
